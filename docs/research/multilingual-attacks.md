# Multilingual prompt injection and jailbreaks: what is known (2023 to 2026)

Written 2026-10-05. Every paper, number and limit below comes from a primary source (arXiv PDF or abstract page, ACL Anthology page, official statistics page) that was opened and read on 2026-10-04 or 2026-10-05. Anything not opened is marked [unverified] or left out. "My arithmetic" means I computed it from a paper's own table. "Estimate" means my judgment, not a fact from a source. No PolyGuard result exists yet, and nothing here describes one.

Companion file: `docs/research/resource_measures.csv` (one row per catalog language, 87 rows).

## 1. Summary: what matters for PolyGuard

1. The old headline (low-resource languages jailbreak GPT-4, Yong et al. 2023) is real but dated. Newer work on 2024 to 2026 models mostly finds small or no gaps for plain translated attacks, and bigger effects only when attacks are combined with other tricks (mixed languages, multi-turn, structural reframing). A null result on PolyGuard's main hypothesis is plausible, and your preregistration already says so.
2. The citation your code uses for "71% machine translation error" is wrong. The number is not in arXiv:2605.18239. It comes from LinguaSafe (arXiv:2508.12733) and is 71% for Bengali LLM translation (Malay was 36%). The 59.8% to 75.8% figure is in 2605.18239, but it includes English, and English rose too (72.7% to 88.1%), so it does not cleanly show a translation effect.
3. The resource tier, your independent variable, has errors: against the Joshi file your catalog cites, Gujarati, Norwegian and Kyrgyz are class 1 (catalog says 4) and Pashto is class 1 (catalog says 2). Fix this before any scan (tier counts would go from 28/24/35 to 25/24/38). It also makes Gujarati, which you wrote yourself, a human-written low-resource language, which is your single most valuable asset against the translation confound.
4. Almost all prior multilingual work tests harmful-content jailbreaks, not prompt injection against an application's system prompt. The closest published work is XSafety (goal hijacking and prompt leaking in 10 languages, 2023) and XIH-Bench (system versus user instruction conflicts in 6 languages, 2026). Nobody I found has tested 5 injection types across dozens of languages with capability controls, which is a defensible niche, but only if you claim measurement, not discovery.
5. Resource measures disagree with each other. Across your 87 languages, rank correlations are about 0.77 to 0.84 between Joshi class, Common Crawl share and Wikipedia size, and only 13 of your 28 "high" languages are "high" by the Common Crawl rule that Deng et al. used. Say which measure you mean, and treat any measure as a proxy, not a cause.

### 2.2 The paper your code cites for the translation confound: arXiv:2605.18239

Marx and Dunaiski, "Multilingual jailbreaking of LLMs using low-resource languages", Stellenbosch University, submitted 18 May 2026, 12 pages, arXiv preprint (not peer reviewed). I read the full PDF.

What it did: Afrikaans, Kiswahili, isiXhosa, isiZulu (plus English baseline). Single-turn prompts from MultiJail and multi-turn conversations from the Multi-turn Human Jailbreak set, machine translated with Google Translate and filtered to the best translations (47 single-turn and 55 multi-turn prompts survived). Models: Gemini 2 flash and flash-lite, GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini, Anthropic 3.5 Haiku, DeepSeek-V3, Grok-3-mini. Human red-teaming: 3 native speakers per language (1 for Kiswahili) chatting with GPT-4o, Gemini 2 flash and Grok, up to 10 turns.

Verification of your numbers:

- **"59.8% to 75.8%": confirmed.** The text says 59.84% (automated translations) and 75.82% (human red-teaming), chi-square 37.412, p below 0.001. Per-language gains in Table 4: Afrikaans +20.04, isiZulu +12.65, isiXhosa +12.31, Kiswahili +0.99 percentage points.
- **"Machine translation error up to 71%": NOT in this paper.** The only "71" in the text is part of "45.71%" (isiXhosa). The paper measures translation quality with BERTScore, METEOR and BLEU on back-translations and never reports an error rate. The real source is LinguaSafe (section 2.3).
- **The 59.8% average includes English.** Table 4's automated column (English 72.73, isiXhosa 45.71, isiZulu 49.09, Kiswahili 62.34, Afrikaans 69.35) has a plain mean of exactly 59.84 (my arithmetic). The human column's plain mean is 72.1, not 75.82, so the paper does not say how its human average was weighted.
- **English also rose under human red-teaming:** 72.73% to 88.05%, +15.32 points, with no translation at all. Human attackers adapt turn by turn; the automated run replays fixed prompts. So a large part of the "human" gain is a better attacker, not a better translation. Relative to the English gain, isiXhosa (+12.31) and isiZulu (+12.65) gained less than English and Kiswahili (+0.99) far less; only Afrikaans (+20.04) gained more (my arithmetic). The paper's claim that translation quality is "the critical factor" is only partly supported by its own design.
- **For newer models, plain translation barely worked.** In single-turn tests only DeepSeek passed 10% harmful (17.4% isiZulu, 13.0% Kiswahili), English had the lowest rate for every model, and language did not significantly predict harm (chi-square 8.07, p = 0.089). The authors contrast this with Deng et al.'s 83.49% for Kiswahili on GPT-3.5-turbo-0613. Multi-turn attacks worked (English 52.7% to 83.6% by model).
- **Other threats to validity (all from the paper):** human sessions are tiny (Table 6: 9 to 26 conversations per language per model). The translation quality filter is relative to each language (mean plus one standard deviation), so kept isiXhosa prompts are lower in absolute quality than kept Afrikaans ones. Correlations of translation quality with jailbreak rate (r = 0.87 to 0.92) state no sample size, and only four languages exist. Non-English replies were classified through back-translation. The conclusion quotes numbers that disagree with Table 4 (77.08%, 59.5%, "28.6% and 30.6%") and swaps which model produced 78.2% and 70.9%. Treat it as suggestive.
- Their resource rule is Common Crawl share below 0.1% (following Deng), crawl CC-MAIN-2025-13. I checked this against the official statistics: Afrikaans 0.0095%, Kiswahili 0.0095%, isiZulu 0.0011%, isiXhosa 0.0007%, all match the CSV's prior-crawl column.

### 2.3 Where "71%" really comes from: LinguaSafe

Ning et al., "LinguaSafe: A Comprehensive Multilingual Safety Benchmark for LLMs", Shanghai AI Laboratory, arXiv:2508.12733v2, 27 Aug 2025. The authors randomly sampled 500 instances each from their Bengali and Malay transcreations (English safety data adapted to the target language) and report: "vanilla LLM translation exhibited substantial error rates of 71% for Bengali and 36% for Malay", reduced "under human inspection" to 12% for Bengali and 3% for Malay after their translate, estimate, refine pipeline (TATER) with human review.

So: 71% is Bengali only (Malay 36%), it is LLM translation (not Google Translate), it is two languages of one benchmark, and "error" follows an MQM-style scheme in which minor errors "do not disrupt the flow or hinder comprehension" (the text I read does not define precisely what counted in the 500-item check). A 71% "contains an error" rate is therefore not the same as "71% of prompts were unreadable" [my inference]. RELATED_WORK.md's "71% to between 3% and 12%" is right in spirit (Bengali 71 to 12, Malay 36 to 3) but should cite 2508.12733.

### 2.5 Did newer frontier models close the gap?

Evidence, in order of strength (all opened):

- **Strongest for "small or no gap on plain translation":** Marx and Dunaiski 2026 (4 African languages, 2024 to 2025 models, language did not significantly predict harm) and Song et al. on GPT-4o (single languages 0% to 6%).
- **For instruction hierarchy (closest to PolyGuard):** XIH-Bench found GPT-5 near the ceiling in all six languages, but weaker models still varied by language by 5 to 14 points. So "closed for the top model, open for smaller models" is the supported statement. PolyGuard's users will often deploy small or cheap models, which matters for your victim choice.
- **Reversals and non-monotone results:** Said and Sani (arXiv:2512.24556v2, 4 Jan 2026): GPT-5.1, Gemini 3 Pro, Anthropic Opus 4.5, English versus Hausa, 1,440 evaluations; Opus 4.5 was "safer" in Hausa (45.0% safe) than English (36.7%), which the authors attribute to "uncertainty-driven refusal", the refused-versus-did-not-understand confound again. Ford et al. (arXiv:2605.23157, Appen): English and Mexican Spanish, four frontier models, 363 scenarios; role-play attacks were weaker in Spanish while image-based attacks were stronger; model rankings changed by language. Addagada (arXiv:2609.08373, draft v1, 8 Sep 2026, one independent author, not reviewed): Gemini 2.5 Flash and Flash-lite, 4 languages, 11 of 12 non-English conditions below English, attributed to lower-quality harmful output in lower-resource languages.
- **Still reported open:** Yong et al. 2025 (arXiv:2505.24119, EMNLP 2025 survey of nearly 300 papers from 2020 to 2024) say the field is English-centric, non-English languages are rarely studied alone, and 50.6% of English-only papers do not name their language. A 2026 literature review of low-resource safety (arXiv:2608.14626, 50 studies) says translated English benchmarks fail to capture culturally rooted harms.
- **Caveat on all of the above:** these tests use different attacks, judges, languages, and models, and most are small. None is a clean before and after on the same attack.

### 2.6 The translation and capability confound, in one place

- Poor machine translation can make an attack fail, which reads as safety (Marx and Dunaiski, section 2.2, with the caveat about English; Yong's own limitations).
- A model that does not understand the language also fails the attack. Yong counted "I don't understand" as a refusal; Shen measured "following rate"; Said and Sani saw a refusal driven by uncertainty; Theocharopoulos saw an Arabic null with no control. PolyGuard's six benign controls per language directly address this and are its strongest design feature.
- In PolyGuard, tier and translation method are fully aligned: per the preregistration the 20 authored languages are 16 high and 4 mid, zero low, so all 35 low-resource languages are machine translated. Any low-versus-high difference is therefore also an authored-versus-machine-translated difference. The controls and the reverse-translation gate reduce this, but cannot remove it. (Correcting Gujarati to class 1, section 2.7, gives you one authored low-resource language, which helps.)
- Extra direction found in XIH-Bench: attacks in a different language from the system prompt are slightly weaker (+3.0 points of preserved system-prompt compliance). So an English system prompt attacked in language X may understate what a same-language deployment would see [inference; effect measured on six large languages only].

### 2.7 How resource level was measured in prior work, and your catalog

Measures used in the papers I read:

- **Joshi et al. 2020** (ACL; https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.560/): six classes 0 to 5 built from labeled data (LDC catalog, ELRA map) and unlabeled data (Wikipedia page counts as the proxy). Class sizes: 0: 2,191 languages, 1: 222, 2: 19, 3: 28, 4: 18, 5: 7. Used by Yong (low = 0 to 2, mid = 3, high = 4 to 5, the same rule as PolyGuard) and Vo et al. (at least one language per level).
- **Common Crawl share**, with thresholds high above 1%, medium 0.1% to 1%, low below 0.1% (Deng et al., following Lai et al. 2023; also LinguaSafe, Li et al., Marx and Dunaiski); Song et al. add 0.01% for "extremely low".
- **Share of a model's own training data**: Upadhayay and Behzadan and Wang et al. quote LLaMA-2's published pretraining language shares (for example Chinese 0.13%); not available for closed models.
- **Wikipedia size**: used by Joshi as a component. Shen et al. define resource level by "general amount of linguistic resources" citing Wu and Dredze 2020 (not opened) and follow the NLLB team's categories.

What I built (CSV): Joshi class (from your catalog and, separately, from the published file), Common Crawl share (latest crawl and the crawl Marx and Dunaiski used), Wikipedia articles and depth. Findings:

- **Catalog errors against the Joshi file you cite** (https://microsoft.github.io/linguisticdiversity/assets/lang2tax.txt, 2,483 rows, whose class counts match Joshi's Table 1 exactly): Gujarati 1 (catalog 4), Norwegian 1 for both Bokmal and Nynorsk (catalog 4), Kirghiz 1 (catalog 4), Pashto 1 (catalog 2). The catalog docstring and PREREGISTRATION.md treat "Kyrgyz = 4" as a Joshi anomaly; per the file it is a transcription error. Cross-check: your catalog has 21 languages in class 4, but Joshi's class 4 has only 18 languages; removing Gujarati, Norwegian and Kyrgyz gives exactly 18. Two more names are ambiguous in the file (Sinhala 0 or Sinhalese 1; Haitian Creole 0 or Haitian 2); both stay "low" either way. With file classes the tiers become 25 high / 24 mid / 38 low.
- **Measures agree only roughly.** Spearman rank correlation over the 87 languages (my computation): Joshi class vs Common Crawl share 0.77 (0.80 using file classes); Joshi vs Wikipedia articles 0.78 (0.81); Common Crawl share vs Wikipedia 0.84.
- **The Common Crawl rule puts most of your languages in "low".** In crawl CC-MAIN-2026-39, 14 of 87 are above 1%, 24 between 0.1% and 1%, 49 below 0.1%. Of your 28 "high" tier languages, only 13 are high by that rule (12 medium, 3 low: Gujarati, Basque, Kyrgyz); 11 of 24 "mid" languages are below 0.1%. A language that is "low resource" for Deng et al. is therefore not the same set as "low tier" for you. Examples: Arabic class 5 but 0.67% of pages; Hindi class 4 but 0.21%.
- **Wikipedia is distorted by bots.** Cebuano has 6,115,881 articles (second only to English) but a Meta "depth" of 2 and about 238 active users; Swedish similar in direction but milder. Do not use raw article counts for Cebuano.
- Common Crawl shares are CLD2 "primary language" percentages of pages (not words or tokens), with unknown-language pages kept in the denominator. CLD2 reports Serbian, Croatian and Bosnian as separate codes, and Norwegian Bokmal and Nynorsk separately, so those shares are not cleanly comparable.

### 2.8 What an 87-language, controls-included, preregistered tool can and cannot claim

Can credibly address:

1. Whether, for a stated victim model and system prompt in 2026, injection success differs across languages by more than chance, using language as the unit and the max-gap permutation test (this avoids the best-of-k illusion behind the 79% headlines).
2. Whether differences track a graded resource measure across many languages, with the measure named in advance (most prior work used 3 to 12 languages and a high/medium/low split).
3. Whether a quiet language is quiet because of defence or because of incomprehension, via the benign controls (no prior paper separates these per language; Shen's "following rate" is the closest).
4. Whether hardening a prompt in one language transfers to held-out phrasings and other languages, with benign follow rates reported.
5. Differences between victim models, including small cheap models, where XIH-Bench says language effects still exist.

Would be overreach:

- "Low-resource languages are less safe", or that you "found" or "showed" a gap: zero live scans exist, and prior work finds mixed results on newer models.
- Any causal statement about resource level. Resource level is entangled with translation quality, script and tokenization, language family, morphology (Song et al.) and model-specific language strengths (XIH-Bench).
- Statements about all chatbots or all vendors from a pilot with a roughly $10 budget (a handful of victims, 5 categories by 3 phrasings per language).
- That the multilingual gap "has closed" or "is still open" for frontier models: no study I found tests this cleanly, and a small pilot cannot either.
- Harmful-content results: PolyGuard measures a harmless canary and prompt leakage, not harmful-content jailbreaks. Do not compare its rates directly with Yong's or Deng's numbers.
- That the 67 machine-translated languages are native-quality, or that Arabic covers dialects (review was MSA only).
- That Joshi class is a measure of how much safety training data a model saw. It is a 2020 proxy built from labeled data catalogs and Wikipedia.
- That PolyGuard is the first multilingual injection tool or the first to use a canary: prior art exists on the injection side (XSafety's goal hijacking and prompt leaking, XIH-Bench), and canary scoring appears in English benchmarks listed in RELATED_WORK.md [not opened by me].
- Name collision: "PolyGuard: A Multilingual Safety Moderation Tool for 17 Languages" (Kumar et al., arXiv:2504.04377, 2025) is a different, published multilingual safety classifier with the same name. A judge who searches will find it. Say so in the README and describe yours as a red-team scanner, not a moderation model.

## Appendix: the CSV (`resource_measures.csv`)

87 rows, one per catalog code. Columns: `code`, `name`, `joshi_class` (from the catalog), `tier_from_catalog`, `tier_by_stated_rule`, `joshi_class_lang2tax_file` (read from the published file), `joshi_catalog_vs_file` (yes, NO, or ambiguous), `cc_share_percent` (share of all pages in crawl `cc_crawl_id` = CC-MAIN-2026-39 whose CLD2 primary language is the language), `cc_cld2_code`, `cc_pages`, `cc_share_percent_prior_crawl` (CC-MAIN-2025-13, the crawl Marx and Dunaiski used), `cc_band_deng2024` (Deng thresholds: above 1% high, 0.1% to 1% medium, below 0.1% low), `wikipedia_articles` and `wikipedia_read_utc` (live API, 2026-10-05), `wikipedia_depth` and `wikipedia_articles_meta_list` (Meta list, same day), `source_notes`.

Coverage: 87 of 87 for every column, no empty cells. Checks: my computed Common Crawl shares match the official published percentages to 4 decimals for all 87 languages in both crawls; Wikipedia article counts from the API and the Meta list differ by under 0.5% everywhere; the Marx and Dunaiski Common Crawl values are reproduced. Limits: CLD2 detection quality on very rare languages is not validated here; Wikipedia counts are not comparable for bot-heavy editions (Cebuano); the Joshi file has duplicate names for Sinhala and Haitian Creole.
